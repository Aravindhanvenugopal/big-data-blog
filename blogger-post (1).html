Petabytes to Pixels

Your dataset grew a thousandfold. Your canvas did not. What actually happens to a chart when the data outnumbers the ink — and four moves that keep the picture honest.

9 min read · beginner friendly · no code required

A chart is arithmetic disguised as a picture. Before a single line of plotting code runs, the screen has already decided how much of your data you are allowed to show. Most of the trouble in modern data visualisation starts with ignoring that decision.

01The chart has a fixed budget

Take an ordinary chart panel: 400 by 300 pixels, 120,000 in total. Now plot five million rows, one mark each. Every pixel of that canvas is carrying roughly forty rows. No amount of colour, transparency or clever styling changes the outcome — the panel fills in, becomes a solid block of ink, and the shape of the data disappears behind its own volume.

This is not a rendering bug. It is a budget problem. A scatter plot's information capacity is not the number of rows you pushed into it; it is the number of marks a reader can still tell apart. Once marks overlap, extra rows stop adding information and start adding weight.

Human vision has the same shape of limit. Colour, position and size differences are picked up almost instantly — in well under a quarter of a second — but only while the number of distinct values stays small. A handful of hues reads instantly. Fifty hues read as mud. The classic experiments by Cleveland and McGill put the visual encodings in a rough order of how accurately people decode them:

Ordered roughly by accuracy of decoding, after Cleveland & McGill (1984)

Position on a common scaleHighestScatter plots, dot plots, any quantitative comparison

The question to ask Not “how much data can I plot?” but “how many marks can a reader still separate?” Everything else follows from the answer.

02Move one: bin before you draw

Binning replaces “one row, one mark” with “one cell, one number”. Square grids, hexagonal grids and two-dimensional histograms all do the same job: divide the plane into a fixed set of containers, count what falls into each, and colour the container by the count. Counts are read from colour ramps far more accurately than overlapping points are read from position.

Hexagons are popular for a specific reason. Every hexagon has six neighbours at the same distance, so the aggregation is less directional than a square grid, and the finished chart looks less like graph paper with numbers in it.

When the axes are latitude and longitude

Do not invent your own grid. A real spatial index keeps cell edges aligned across datasets, so counts from two different sources — two years, two cities, two agencies — can be compared without re-deriving geometry. Uber's H3 assigns every point on Earth to a hexagonal cell at one of sixteen resolutions; the same cell identifier means the same patch of ground no matter who computed it.

What changes when you change the cell size
Cell sizeYou gainYou lose
Too small (a few rows per cell)Fine texture and local detailReal pattern drowns in noise; empty cells look like absences
Roughly right (dozens of rows per cell)Stable counts, visible structureVery little — this is the working zone
Too largeA calm, smooth pictureDistinct clusters merge; gradients appear that were never in the data

A practical test: choose a cell size, draw the chart, then halve and double it. If the story survives all three, it is in the data. If it changes completely, it was in the bin size.

Worth remembering Aggregation is an argument, not a neutral step. Choosing a cell size is choosing what counts as one observation — and therefore what the chart is allowed to say.

03Move two: downsample time series honestly

The naive fix for a big time series is to fetch fewer rows: the first thousand of them, or every hundredth one. Both are sampling, and sampling has a failure mode with a name — aliasing. A spike that lasts thirty seconds can fall entirely between your samples, and no reader will ever know it existed.

M4, published by Jugel, Jerzak, Hackenbroich and Markl in 2013, does exactly what the bottom panel shows: four numbers per pixel column — first, last, minimum, maximum. A 1,000-pixel-wide chart needs at most 4,000 numbers whether the source holds a thousand rows or a billion, and the extremes are preserved by construction rather than by luck.

Largest-Triangle-Three-Buckets, from Sveinn Steinarsson's 2013 thesis, is the other common choice. It keeps the visual shape of a line using very few points, which is excellent for dashboards and sparklines. But it selects individual representative points rather than extremes, so a narrow spike can still slip away. Use LTTB when shape is the message; use M4 when the extremes are the message.

The rule underneath both: the sampler has to be chosen by what the chart claims. If your caption says “load never exceeded 200 requests per second”, your sampler had better preserve maxima — otherwise the caption is describing a number your pipeline invented.

04Move three: send bytes, not rows

Every interactive chart has a second budget: the network. Five million rows carrying a timestamp, a latitude, a longitude and a value comes to 160 MB as raw 8-byte numbers, roughly 300 MB as JSON text, and a long wait on a phone.

Columnar formats change that arithmetic. Parquet stores each column separately, so timestamps compress as deltas and repeated values as dictionary codes; the same five million rows typically lands in the tens of megabytes. Columnar files are also splittable, which means a browser can request only the byte ranges it needs for the columns and row groups it will actually draw.

What the reader's browser actually downloads
ApproachPayloadGood when
PNG export of the chart~0.2 MBStatic article; nothing to explore
Binned aggregates from the server0.1–2 MBOne fixed view, no drill-down
Parquet + DuckDB-WASM10–40 MBFiltering, brushing, re-aggregation in the browser
JSON or CSV of every row160–300 MBOnly when the reader genuinely needs every row

DuckDB compiled to WebAssembly runs a real SQL engine inside the browser and reads Parquet over HTTP range requests, so the heavy work happens after download and only the aggregate leaves the query. Datashader takes the other road: it rasterises hundreds of millions of points on the server into a pixel grid, and the browser receives an image plus axes rather than data. WebGL libraries such as deck.gl push the drawing itself onto the GPU.

Whichever you choose, the principle is the same: the reader's browser is a compute node you already have. Ship the compressed source and the query, not the rendered rows.

Aggregation is a promise to the reader. Every chart is a claim about what was thrown away.

05Move four: don't let the aggregate speak for the parts

The last failure is not perceptual but statistical, and it survives every rendering trick. Two models are evaluated on the same benchmark, split into hard and easy questions:

Two models, same benchmark, opposite conclusions
ModelHard questionsEasy questionsOverall
Model A40.0% (40 / 100)75.0% (300 / 400)68.0% (340 / 500)
Model B30.0% (3 / 10)70.0% (700 / 1000)69.6% (703 / 1010)

Model A wins on hard questions and on easy questions, and still loses overall — because Model B was mostly asked easy questions. This is Simpson's paradox, and a single bar chart labelled “accuracy” will report the reversal with complete confidence and no warning.

Charts make this mistake faster than tables do, because a bar hides its own composition. The defences are cheap: print the group sizes next to the percentages, break the aggregate into small multiples with one panel per stratum, and treat any single-number comparison as a summary that needs its denominators nearby.

Two smaller cousins of the same problem are worth naming. A log axis compresses large values so aggressively that a doubling looks like a gentle slope; it is often the honest choice, but only when the label says so. And an axis that starts at 60 instead of zero can turn a three per cent change into a cliff — acceptable on a line chart tracking one series, misleading on bars, whose length is the encoding.

06How the pipeline works, in plain English

None of this needs you to write a program, and no reader of your chart needs to know a query language. Every tool that handles big data well does the same four things, in the same order:

  1. Store it once, compactly. Keep the full dataset as a compressed columnar file rather than re-exporting spreadsheets. Parquet is the common choice, and it is what lets everything below stay small.
  2. Aggregate before you draw. Ask for the cells you are actually going to paint — a couple of thousand counts, not five million rows. The grouping is the whole trick; the rest is presentation.
  3. Fetch only the slice on screen. A chart of one city in one month should download that city and that month, not the country and the decade. Columnar files allow exactly this, because each column can be read on its own.
  4. Draw at screen resolution. One mark per position the eye can distinguish, coloured by the count in that position — the visual decision from section one, applied at the end.

The first two steps take seconds on an ordinary laptop. DuckDB, a free database that runs as a single file, reads Parquet directly and does the binning and counting in one pass; pandas and R do the same job with more memory and less speed. The third step is what makes a chart open quickly on a phone. The fourth is the only step the reader ever sees.

For a college project the practical version is simpler still. Keep your data in one CSV file, group it by your chosen cell in whatever tool you already use, save the aggregated table, and plot that. The finished chart is identical to the one a production pipeline would produce — the only difference is where the aggregation happened.

That trade is the whole discipline in one sentence: compute once, ship small, and render only what the eye can resolve. A chart that arrives in a second and says something true is worth more than one that arrives in a minute and says nothing.

07Six checks before you publish

  1. Count your pixels. If the row count is larger than the pixel count of the panel, you are not drawing data, you are painting. Bin it or rasterise it.
  2. Halve and double the bin. If the story changes when the cell size changes, the story was the cell size.
  3. Preserve extremes. Any sampler that can drop a spike must say so in the caption — or be replaced by one that cannot.
  4. Show the denominators. Percentages without group sizes are an open invitation to Simpson's paradox.
  5. Label the scale. Log axes, truncated axes and dual axes are all legitimate. Silent ones are not.
  6. Say what was aggregated. One line under the figure: source, period, unit, cell size, and what a single mark represents.
Three exercises Take any 100,000-row CSV and plot it at three bin sizes. Find a live dashboard whose y-axis starts above zero and describe what it does to the story. Then rebuild the two-model table above from your own data — it is the fastest way to stop trusting a single summary number.
